An options overlay writes or buys listed options on your portfolio. Writing calls caps your upside in exchange for premium; buying puts costs premium that is lost if the market does not fall. Options can be exercised or assigned early, can be hard to trade at a fair price, and have tax rules of their own (including straddle and Section 1256 rules). # Options overlay — risks and disclosures

*Default — have counsel review.*

## What it is

An options overlay adds listed options to a portfolio — for example selling covered calls for income, or buying
protective puts. The options sleeve is managed alongside the stock portfolio and is executed manually.

## Risks

- **Capped upside.** Writing a call gives up gains above the strike price for the term of the option.
- **Premium lost.** A bought option expires worthless if the market does not move past its strike.
- **Early exercise and assignment.** American-style options can be assigned at any time, which can force a sale of
  the underlying stock and realize a gain.
- **Liquidity and pricing.** Some options trade thinly; prices can move quickly and spreads can be wide.
- **Leverage.** Options can magnify losses as well as gains.

## Tax

Options have their own tax treatment. Index options may be Section 1256 contracts (marked to market, 60 % long-term
/ 40 % short-term). Options combined with stock positions can create straddles, which can defer losses and change
holding periods. Confirm the treatment with your tax adviser.

## Approval and the options disclosure document

Your custodian must approve the account for the level of options trading used. Read *Characteristics and Risks of
Standardized Options* (the Options Clearing Corporation's disclosure document) before trading.
